title: "The Inflation Argument Against a New Pay Scale Is Its Own Worst Enemy"
date: 2026-06-07
last_updated: June 2026
description: "Postponing pay-scale revisions is what turns them into the inflationary shock critics fear."
tags: [Macroeconomics, Public Finance, Bangladesh]
---

There is a paradox inside the inflation argument against revising the pay scale. The argument says: do not raise salaries now, because it will push prices higher. But every time that argument has won, it has made the eventual revision larger, more abrupt, and more genuinely difficult to absorb. The fiscal conservatism that postpones the correction is precisely what guarantees, when the correction finally arrives, that it arrives as a shock. The inflation hawks have been defeating themselves.

Think about what postponement actually does. If the pay scale had been adjusted modestly every two or three years in line with inflation, no single revision would ever be large enough to worry about. A five or seven percent adjustment spread across the budget is a rounding error in aggregate demand. Nobody writes alarm pieces about it. But freeze wages for a decade while prices nearly double, and now the arithmetic of catch-up demands something like a forty or fifty percent revision all at once, concentrated in a single fiscal year, hitting the budget as a lump. The demand impulse that critics fear is not a property of salary revision. It is a property of delay. Every year the argument against revision wins, it is quietly adding to the size of the shock it claims to be preventing.

The government's proposed three-phase implementation over two to three years softens this somewhat, but a revision spread across three large tranches is still categorically different from the small incremental adjustments that would never have generated this debate in the first place.

The inflation concern also misidentifies the source of Bangladesh's price problem. IMF data found that roughly half of the CPI increase in FY2023 was attributable to exchange rate depreciation alone, with global commodity prices and domestic fuel adjustments accounting for most of the rest. Look at the composition of that inflation: Taka depreciation, import cost pass-through, energy price adjustments, supply chain disruption. None of these have anything to do with the wages of government clerks. Blaming a salary revision for an inflation that was already entrenched before the revision exists is not an analytical position. It is a narrative looking for a villain.

The more serious version of the concern invokes a wage-price spiral: wages go up, costs pass through to prices, workers demand more wages, and so the cycle feeds itself. This image is compelling. It is also, historically, rare. A comprehensive IMF study examining spiral episodes across advanced economies since the 1960s found that only about a quarter of cases where wages and prices rose together were followed by a sustained acceleration in both. In most episodes, nominal wages and prices stabilized and reverted, with real wages eventually recovering their lost ground. The spiral is the exception, not the template.

Why? Because a spiral does not run on wages alone. It needs the central bank to lose control of the dynamic — to keep expanding the money supply and validate each round of price increases rather than tightening to break the loop. In practice, central banks do tighten, which is precisely why spirals are the exception and not the rule. Lorenzoni and Werning, writing in the Brookings Papers in 2023, show formally that even where a wage-price feedback exists, whether it spirals or stabilizes depends entirely on monetary policy credibility. The inflation argument against the pay scale is therefore an argument about whether Bangladesh Bank can be trusted to hold the line or not, and what our central bank has actually been doing to curb the pressure. That implication is almost never stated openly. The debate should be where it actually belongs: the independence and effectiveness of monetary policy, not the pay commission.

There is a final point that cuts deeper than any of the above. Before worrying about the demand impulse, consider who we are actually talking about. According to government data, civil servants and affiliated public sector workers together amount to roughly three percent of Bangladesh's total employed workforce. The idea that adjusting the wages of three percent of the workforce will meaningfully destabilize aggregate prices requires assuming that this group's additional income gets spent very rapidly, very densely, and in very large quantities all at once. That is not impossible. But it is precisely the kind of marginal demand impulse that a functioning central bank should be able to manage without difficulty, and many developing country central banks have done exactly that.

Then there is the more basic philosophical problem with the ten-year revision cycle that no macroeconomic framing quite captures. A civil servant joins the service, earns a workable wage for a few years, then watches the cost of living quietly overtake their salary for the better part of a decade, and is expected to remain honest, effective, and professionally committed throughout. There is no compensation for the years of erosion. The eventual revision does not give back what was lost — it only resets the clock for the next cycle of decline. You cannot ask people to absorb years of real income loss and then act as though a future correction makes them whole. The damage to morale, to institutional trust, and to the quiet daily calculations people make about whether the job is still worth doing — that accumulates in ways that do not appear in any budget line, and cannot be undone by a belated revision alone.

The debate Bangladesh needs is not whether to revise the pay scale. That question has a clear answer. The debate it needs is why it keeps arriving at this point every decade, and what institutional design would replace a ten-year accumulation of grievance with a standing mechanism for incremental real wage maintenance. A cost-of-living adjustment tied to a credible price index, reviewed annually and implemented in small tranches every two to three years, makes the inflation concern essentially moot. A three percent regular adjustment generates almost no controversy. The controversy only exists because the correction has been allowed to accumulate to the point where fifteen percent or more per tranche is now the only arithmetic that works. Whatever the path forward, the central bank's role remains critical and non-negotiable in any scenario.

That is not a macroeconomic problem. It is a political economy failure, and postponing the revision one more time does not solve it. It only deepens it.
